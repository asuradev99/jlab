\section{Theory}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Nuclear Magnetic Resonance}

Atomic nuclei with non-zero spin carry a magnetic moment
$\boldsymbol{\mu} = \gamma\hbar\mathbf{I}$,
where $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio and $\mathbf{I}$ is the
dimensionless spin operator.  In an external static field
$\mathbf{B}_0 = B_0\hat{z}$ the energy eigenstates split by
$\Delta E = \hbar\gamma B_0$, and at thermal equilibrium a small net
magnetisation $M_0\propto B_0/T$ builds up along $\hat{z}$.
The ensemble magnetisation $\mathbf{M}$ precesses about $\hat{z}$ at
the \emph{Larmor frequency}
\begin{equation}
  \omega_L = \gamma B_0.
\end{equation}
For protons (${}^1$H), $\gamma/2\pi \approx \SI{42.6}{\mega\hertz\per\tesla}$;
at the lab field of $B_0\approx\SI{0.177}{\tesla}$ this gives
$\omega_L/2\pi\approx\SI{7.5}{\mega\hertz}$.

Applying a transverse radiofrequency (RF) field oscillating at
$\omega\approx\omega_L$ for a short pulse of duration $t_p$ rotates
$\mathbf{M}$ by angle $\alpha = \gamma B_1 t_p$ away from $\hat{z}$.
A \emph{90° pulse} ($\alpha=\pi/2$) rotates $\mathbf{M}$ entirely into
the transverse plane, where it precesses and induces an oscillating EMF
in the pick-up coil.  This voltage decays as the spins dephase —
the \emph{free induction decay} (FID).

\subsection{Relaxation}

Two independent mechanisms restore the system to thermal equilibrium
after a pulse.

\paragraph{Spin--lattice relaxation ($T_1$).}
The longitudinal component recovers as
\begin{equation}
  M_z(t) = M_0\!\left(1 - e^{-t/T_1}\right),
  \label{eq:T1}
\end{equation}
driven by fluctuating magnetic fields from molecular motion that allow
spins to exchange energy with the lattice.  $T_1$ sets the minimum
wait time between successive pulse sequences; if the repeat time
$T_R \ll T_1$ the magnetisation is partially saturated and the
apparent signal amplitude is suppressed by a factor $(1-e^{-T_R/T_1})$.

\paragraph{Spin--spin relaxation ($T_2$).}
The transverse component decays as
\begin{equation}
  M_{xy}(t) = M_0\,e^{-t/T_2},
  \label{eq:T2}
\end{equation}
caused by randomly fluctuating local dipole fields from neighbouring
nuclei inducing small shifts in each spin's precession frequency.
These random frequency offsets cause the spins to fan out
in the transverse plane, irreversibly destroying the phase coherence
of the ensemble.  The observed FID envelope decays with the shorter
time constant $T_2^*$ defined by
$1/T_2^* = 1/T_2 + \gamma\Delta B_0$,
where $\Delta B_0$ is the static field inhomogeneity across the sample.

\subsection{The Spin-Echo Sequence}

The $T_2^*$ contribution from static field inhomogeneity is reversible
and can be removed with the \emph{Hahn spin-echo} sequence:
\begin{equation*}
  90_x^\circ - \tau - 180_x^\circ - \tau - \text{echo}.
\end{equation*}
The 90° pulse creates transverse magnetisation; during $\tau$ the spins
precess at slightly different rates and dephase.  The 180° pulse
mirrors all spin phases about the $x'$-axis, reversing their relative
ordering.  After a further $\tau$ the spins rephase coherently,
producing a signal maximum (the \emph{echo}) at time $2\tau$.

Because the 180° pulse also reverses the dephasing from static
inhomogeneity, the echo amplitude is insensitive to $\Delta B_0$ and
reflects only true spin--spin dephasing and diffusion:
\begin{equation}
  E(2\tau) = E_0\exp\!\left(
    -\frac{2\tau}{T_2} - \frac{2}{3}\gamma^2 G^2 D\,\tau^3
  \right),
  \label{eq:echo}
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the field gradient and $D$ is the translational diffusion
constant.  For small $\tau$ the cubic diffusion term is negligible and
$E\propto e^{-2\tau/T_2}$, providing a clean measurement of $T_2$.

\subsection{Beeswax as an NMR Sample}

Beeswax is a complex organic mixture of long-chain esters, hydrocarbons,
and fatty acids.  Its proton NMR signal is dominated by the dense
network of CH$_2$ and CH$_3$ groups along the carbon chains.  The
material passes through three thermal phases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Solid (crystalline):} $T \lesssim 40\,^\circ$C.
    Chains are packed into ordered lamellae; rigid lattice
    severely restricts molecular motion.
  \item \textbf{Semicrystalline:} $40\,^\circ\text{C} \lesssim T
    \lesssim 62\,^\circ$C.
    Crystalline and amorphous domains coexist.
  \item \textbf{Liquid:} $T \gtrsim 62\,^\circ$C.
    Long-range order is lost; rapid isotropic tumbling averages
    many dipolar interactions.
\end{itemize}

In the fast-motion (extreme-narrowing) limit, rapid molecular tumbling
averages the static dipole--dipole coupling and drives $T_2\to T_1$.
As temperature increases and molecular dynamics speed up, one
therefore expects $T_2$ to lengthen.
In the rigid-lattice limit, by contrast, static dipolar couplings
are strong and $T_2$ can be extremely short
($\sim\SI{10}{\micro\second}$).
Beeswax at our temperatures ($45$--$70\,^\circ$C) straddles these
regimes, making it an interesting system to probe with NMR.

\section{Apparatus}
% ================================================================

The NMR spectrometer is built around a permanent magnet producing
$B_0\approx\SI{0.177}{\tesla}$.  An Agilent 8313A frequency synthesiser
generates a CW signal near $\omega_L$; a double-balanced mixer (DBM)
gates it into pulses under control of a custom pulse programmer.  A
\SI{2}{\watt} RF power amplifier drives the probe, which consists of an
LC resonant circuit tuned and impedance-matched to $\SI{50}{\ohm}$ at
$\omega_L$.  Crossed-diode protection on a quarter-wavelength
transmission line isolates the preamplifier (Mini-Circuits ZEL-2060A+)
from the high-power transmit path; the diodes are non-conducting during
transmission and present a high impedance to the preamp.  After
amplification, the signal passes through a bandpass filter
($\approx\SI{7.5}{\mega\hertz}$ centre) and is mixed with the
synthesiser reference to produce a low-frequency beat signal displayed
on an oscilloscope.

The beeswax sample was held in a standard \SI{10}{\milli\metre} NMR
glass tube inside the RF coil and repositioned to the region of most
uniform field between measurements.  Temperature was monitored
non-invasively with a FLIR C5 compact thermal camera aimed at the
sample tube; no contact thermometer was used during acquisition so as
not to disturb the field environment.

% ================================================================
\section{Experimental Procedure}
% ================================================================

The beeswax was melted on a hot plate to $\approx70\,^\circ$C and then
allowed to cool naturally to $\approx45\,^\circ$C with the sample
sitting in the magnet bore.  At each of the five pulse spacings
$\tau\in\{2,6,10,16,18\}\,\si{\milli\second}$ a Hahn spin-echo
sequence was run continuously while the sample cooled, and the
peak-to-peak echo amplitude was recorded at
$\Delta T\approx1\,^\circ$C intervals by photographing the
oscilloscope screen.  Each scope display was averaged over 16
successive shots to reduce noise before being captured.

The 90° and 180° pulse widths were determined at the start of each
session by finding the pulse width that minimised the FID amplitude
(giving $t_{180}$) and halving it.  Values of $t_{90} = 31\pm2\,\mu$s
and $t_{180} = 62\pm5\,\mu$s were used.  The repeat time $T_R$ was
set individually for each $\tau$ channel:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc}
\toprule
$\tau$ (ms) & $T_R$ (ms) \\
\midrule
2  & 130 \\
6  & 220 \\
10 & 180 \\
16 & 280 \\
18 & 220 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
These repeat times were chosen to keep the echo observable on a
convenient oscilloscope time-base; they were not scaled to $T_1$ and
therefore vary across channels in a way that introduces a systematic
amplitude bias (see Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

\section{Data Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Amplitude Extraction and Pixel Calibration}

Oscilloscope photographs were taken at each $(\tau, T)$ combination.
The oscilloscope displayed both the spin echo waveform and a
calibration bar of known amplitude, rendered by the scope's internal
graticule system, at the bottom of every screen capture.
Amplitudes were measured in pixels from the photographs, giving a
raw peak-to-peak amplitude $A_\mathrm{px}$ with uncertainty
$\sigma_\mathrm{px}$ estimated by the pixel-level measurement
precision.  Separately, the calibration bar height in pixels
$C_\mathrm{px}$ (with uncertainty $\sigma_{C_\mathrm{px}}$) and its
known value in millivolts $C_\mathrm{mV}$ were recorded.

Because the oscilloscope vertical scale was adjusted independently for
each $\tau$ channel to keep the echo on screen, a separate calibration
factor
\begin{equation}
  s_k = \frac{C_{\mathrm{mV},k}}{C_{\mathrm{px},k}}
  \label{eq:scale}
\end{equation}
was computed for each channel $k \in \{2,6,10,16,18\}\,\mathrm{ms}$.
The calibration values were:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
$\tau$ (ms) & $C_\mathrm{px}$ & $C_\mathrm{mV}$ \\
\midrule
2  & 266 & 5.44 \\
6  & 266 & 5.44 \\
10 & 441 & 23.0 \\
16 & 441 & 23.0 \\
18 & 263 & 13.6 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The amplitude in millivolts is then $A_\mathrm{mV} = A_\mathrm{px}\cdot s_k$,
and the combined fractional uncertainty is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\sigma_A}{A} =
  \sqrt{\!\left(\frac{\sigma_{A_\mathrm{px}}}{A_\mathrm{px}}\right)^{\!2}
       +\left(\frac{\sigma_{C_\mathrm{px}}}{C_\mathrm{px}}\right)^{\!2}},
  \label{eq:Aerr}
\end{equation}
where the known calibration mV value $C_\mathrm{mV}$ is treated as
exact.

\subsection{Fit Region Selection}

A naive application of the spin-echo decay model
$A(\tau)=A_0 e^{-2\tau/T_2}$ to all five $\tau$ values fails because
the repeat times $T_R$ differ across channels.  The echo amplitude
from a sequence with repeat time $T_R$ is proportional to
\begin{equation}
  A \propto \left(1-e^{-T_R/T_1}\right)e^{-2\tau/T_2},
  \label{eq:sat}
\end{equation}
so channels with short $T_R$ relative to $T_1$ are systematically
suppressed.  As beeswax approaches its melting point, $T_1$ grows
large (approaching the liquid-state limit $T_1\sim T_2\sim
\SI{100}{\milli\second}$), making the saturation factor
$(1-e^{-T_R/T_1})$ strongly channel-dependent.  In particular the
$\tau=2\,\mathrm{ms}$ channel ($T_R=130\,\mathrm{ms}$) is far more
saturated than the $\tau=16\,\mathrm{ms}$ channel
($T_R=280\,\mathrm{ms}$), producing a spurious apparent rise in
amplitude from short to long $\tau$ that is unrelated to $T_2$.

Additionally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo} shows that diffusion contributions
grow as $\tau^3$; they are negligible at large $\tau$ where the
$T_2$ term dominates.  For both reasons, fits were restricted to the
three longest pulse spacings, $\tau \in \{10, 16, 18\}\,\mathrm{ms}$,
which form a monotonically decaying sequence for most temperatures and
sit in a regime where diffusion is small.

\subsection{Exponential Decay Fitting}

For each temperature $T_i$, the three amplitudes
$\{A_{10},A_{16},A_{18}\}$ with uncertainties
$\{\sigma_{10},\sigma_{16},\sigma_{18}\}$ were fit to
\begin{equation}
  A(\tau) = A_0\,e^{-2\tau/T_2}
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
using non-linear least squares (\texttt{scipy.optimize.curve\_fit})
with \texttt{absolute\_sigma=True} so that the reported covariance
matrix reflects the physical uncertainties rather than the scatter.
The initial guess was $A_0^{(0)}=A_{10}\exp(2\tau_{10}/15)$,
$T_2^{(0)}=15\,\mathrm{ms}$, which places the starting point in the
correct order of magnitude for liquid beeswax.

With $N=3$ data points and $p=2$ free parameters ($A_0$ and $T_2$),
the fit has $\nu = N-p = 1$ degree of freedom.  The uncertainty on
$T_2$ is taken as $\sigma_{T_2} = \sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{22}}$ where
$\mathcal{C}$ is the covariance matrix returned by \texttt{curve\_fit}.

\subsection{Goodness-of-Fit Assessment}

The chi-squared statistic is
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2 = \sum_{k}\left(\frac{A_k - A(\tau_k)}{\sigma_{A_k}}\right)^{\!2},
\end{equation}
with the sum over the three fitted $\tau$ values.  The $p$-value
$p = P(\chi^2_1 > \chi^2_\mathrm{obs})$ is evaluated from the
$\chi^2$ distribution with $\nu=1$ degree of freedom using
\texttt{scipy.stats.chi2.sf}.  A small $p$-value indicates that the
residuals are larger than expected from the stated measurement
uncertainties, signalling either underestimated errors or model
inadequacy.

\subsection{Temperature Uncertainty}

The thermal camera records temperature at discrete frames separated by
$\Delta t = \SI{1}{\second}$.  A $\SI{1}{\second}$ timing uncertainty
in associating a camera frame with the moment of echo acquisition
propagates to a temperature uncertainty
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_T = \left|\frac{dT}{dt}\right|\Delta t.
\end{equation}
The cooling rate $|dT/dt|$ varies during the run and differs slightly
between $\tau$ channels (because each channel was run in a separate
cooling trial).  A calibration dataset (\texttt{beeswax\_temp\_calibration.csv})
recorded $T_\mathrm{cam}(t)$ continuously during five independent
cooling runs, one per $\tau$ value.

For each calibration run the derivative was estimated numerically
using central finite differences at interior temperature points,
\begin{equation}
  \left.\frac{dT}{dt}\right|_i
    = \frac{T_{i+1}-T_{i-1}}{t_{i+1}-t_{i-1}},
  \label{eq:diff}
\end{equation}
and one-sided differences at the endpoints.  The resulting
$|dT/dt|(T)$ curve was interpolated to each measurement temperature
$T_i$ using linear interpolation.  The per-channel estimates were
then averaged to give a single uncertainty estimate at each $T_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_T(T_i) = \Delta t\cdot
  \frac{1}{N_\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{N_\tau}
  \left|\frac{dT}{dt}\right|_k(T_i),
  \label{eq:sigT}
\end{equation}
with $N_\tau=5$.

\section{Results}
% ================================================================

  Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} to the green-shaded region

  \label{fig:amp}

Figure~\ref{fig:amp} shows the calibrated amplitudes alongside the
fitted decay curves.  The data reveal two qualitatively distinct
regimes.

\textit{Low temperature ($T\lesssim49\,^\circ$C).}  The amplitudes
decrease monotonically with $\tau$ across all five channels, and the
exponential fits to $\tau=10,16,18\,\mathrm{ms}$ have
$\chi^2\lesssim1.4$ (Table~\ref{tab:chi2}), consistent with the
measurement uncertainties.  The model describes the data well.

\textit{High temperature ($T\gtrsim50\,^\circ$C).}  The amplitude
at $\tau=16\,\mathrm{ms}$ exceeds that at $\tau=10\,\mathrm{ms}$
for most high-temperature measurements, producing a non-monotone
pattern that is incompatible with pure $T_2$ decay.  The $\chi^2$
values increase sharply, reaching $\sim40$--$55$ at
$57$--$70\,^\circ$C ($\nu=1$), and the $p$-values are effectively
zero.

The origin of the high-temperature anomaly is the variation in repeat
time across $\tau$ channels (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sat}).  As temperature
rises toward and above $62\,^\circ$C, beeswax melts and $T_1$
increases substantially.  With $T_1\gg T_R$, the $(1-e^{-T_R/T_1})$
prefactor differentially suppresses short-$\tau$ channels (small $T_R$)
relative to long-$\tau$ channels (large $T_R$), causing the apparent
amplitude to \emph{increase} with $\tau$ at short spacings — exactly
the pattern seen in the data.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{bwax_T2_vs_Tcam.pdf}
  \caption{Fitted $T_2$ vs.\ camera temperature.  Horizontal error
  bars are the timing-derived temperature uncertainties of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigT}; vertical error bars are $1\sigma$ from the
  covariance matrix.  Only the region $T\lesssim49\,^\circ$C
  (statistically acceptable fits) is physically interpretable.}
  \label{fig:T2}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:T2} shows $T_2$ vs.\ $T_\mathrm{cam}$.  In the
low-temperature range where fits are statistically acceptable
($T\lesssim49\,^\circ$C), $T_2$ lies in the range
$\sim100$--$200\,\mathrm{ms}$ and shows no strong trend within
uncertainties.  At higher temperatures the fitted $T_2$ values are
spuriously small and scatter widely, a direct consequence of the
saturation-induced amplitude bias: when the amplitude at
$\tau=16\,\mathrm{ms}$ is anomalously large relative to
$\tau=18\,\mathrm{ms}$, the least-squares fit must produce a steep
slope (short $T_2$) to accommodate both points.

The temperature uncertainty $\sigma_T$ computed from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:diff}--\eqref{eq:sigT} is dominated by the cooling
rate, which averages $\approx0.2\,^\circ\mathrm{C\,s}^{-1}$
throughout the run, giving $\sigma_T\approx0.2\,^\circ$C.  This is
small compared to the $1\,^\circ$C spacing between measurement
temperatures, so timing errors are not a dominant uncertainty source.
A more significant systematic is the gap between the camera surface
temperature $T_\mathrm{cam}$ and the true bulk sample temperature,
which may differ by several degrees depending on the tube wall
thickness and thermal equilibration.

\section{Conclusion}
% ================================================================

We measured spin-echo amplitudes in beeswax at five pulse spacings
across the temperature range $45$--$70\,^\circ$C and extracted $T_2$
by fitting an exponential decay model to the three longest spacings
($\tau=10,16,18\,\mathrm{ms}$) at each temperature.

The goodness-of-fit analysis reveals a clear systematic failure above
$\approx50\,^\circ$C: the $\chi^2$ values are one to two orders of
magnitude above expectation, driven by a saturation bias arising from
the channel-dependent repeat time $T_R$.  In the melt phase, where
$T_1$ grows large, the fixed $T_R$ values suppress short-$\tau$
amplitudes relative to long-$\tau$ amplitudes, creating a spurious
apparent rise in the data that the $T_2$-decay model cannot fit.

In the solid/semicrystalline region ($T\lesssim49\,^\circ$C) the
fits are statistically acceptable and indicate $T_2\sim100$--$200\,\mathrm{ms}$.
No firm conclusion about the temperature dependence of $T_2$ can be
drawn from this limited range without either (a) scaling $T_R$
independently at each temperature to satisfy $T_R\gg T_1$, or (b)
measuring $T_1(T)$ directly and correcting each amplitude via
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sat}.  Both improvements are straightforward with the
existing apparatus and would allow the full $T_2(T)$ curve across
the phase transition to be measured reliably.
